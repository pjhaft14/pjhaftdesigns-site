Hitachi · Disney ride maintenance

Helping theme park engineers prevent ride downtime and guest frustration.

I led UX design for a ride-maintenance experience for Space Mountain at Walt Disney World, giving engineers a clearer path from component-health alerts to maintenance planning. The design brings health readings, trends, and component locations together on tablets and phones, then carries that context into work and parts requests. The goal was to help engineers investigate earlier and coordinate maintenance around park operations to reduce unexpected ride downtime.

The problem

Engineers received alerts after ride problems occurred.

During our on-site Disney visit, the engineer we spoke with described a reactive workflow: engineers learned about issues through email after something had gone wrong. Limited sensor coverage meant the notification pointed to an affected ride area, rather than the exact faulty component, and email delivery depended on network connectivity.

Engineers then had to investigate the area, identify the component, and determine the repair before requesting replacement parts through a separate email exchange. When a ride stopped during park hours, that investigation and repair process interrupted operations, contributed to longer queues, and frustrated guests.

They needed proactive access to component health and location wherever they were working, with enough evidence to investigate earlier and carry the findings into work and parts requests. That context would help them assess what needed immediate attention and what could be addressed during overnight maintenance.

Field research

Put component health in the engineer's hands.

I led research and persona interviews, including an on-site visit to Disney where we spoke with an engineer about daily routines and the information they monitored.

Our team's central finding was the need for proactive access to component health on portable devices such as tablets and phones. Engineers needed to check status while moving through the ride environment, rather than wait for an email to tell them something had gone wrong.

Design challenge

Make the next step clear. Keep the engineer in control.

More sensors could provide more data, but not an automatic answer. The experience needed to connect the affected component, evidence of change, and a practical next step.

A prediction must not look like a guaranteed failure time. Proposed overnight work is not an approved schedule, and requesting a part does not confirm availability. The design needed to make those boundaries clear while helping engineers coordinate the response.

Design decisions

Four choices shaped the experience.

Make status easy to check

02

Connect health to location

Considered
Show health readings separately from the ride map.
Chose

03

Bring timing into the decision

Considered
Show component condition without maintenance timing.
Chose
Show the next maintenance window and a path to escalate urgent issues.

Show the next maintenance window alongside the component's condition and a path to escalate urgent issues.

04

Considered

Chose

Story 01

Review the alert and assess the issue

The walkthrough follows a flagged brake actuator from the alert list into its component details, where location, readings, and trend data are available alongside the next maintenance window. The engineer can review the issue without losing the wider ride context.

I designed this step to support an assessment before a maintenance decision. Engineers can compare the component’s condition with normal behavior, consider timing, and choose whether to create a work request or escalate for immediate engineering review.

Story 02

Propose the maintenance work

Once the engineer decides to propose maintenance, the same component context carries into the work request. Location and health evidence are already attached, while the work description, notes, and proposed maintenance window can be adjusted to reflect the engineer’s assessment.

The walkthrough shows an overnight maintenance proposal being submitted as a shared record for engineering review. The intent was to turn the assessment into a clear handoff, with the component evidence available to the team confirming the work and timing.

Story 03

Request the part and see what is pending

After the work request is submitted, the engineer can continue directly into a linked parts request. The affected component and work request stay connected while the engineer specifies quantity, needed-by time, and delivery location.

The final view brings engineering review, parts confirmation, and proposed timing together so the engineer can see what is still pending. This gives maintenance and parts teams a shared reference for coordinating the repair, with compatibility, availability, and delivery timing left for the parts team to confirm.

Behind the design

The details that support an engineer's next decision

At-a-glance context

Put what needs attention beside the next maintenance window

I grouped monitoring coverage, issues needing review, vehicle activity, and the next overnight window into one summary strip. Engineers can orient themselves before looking into a specific component.

Neutral counts provide context, red emphasizes issues needing attention, and blue distinguishes the planning window. Labels and the priority breakdown make those differences clear without relying on color alone.

Location and priority

Connect the alert to its location

The original email identified a broad ride area. I connected a prioritized alert list to the ride schematic so engineers could identify the component, understand its priority, and locate it without losing the wider context.

Understanding the issue

Give the engineer evidence for the next decision

The drawer organizes the evidence around the engineer's assessment, from identifying the component to choosing a response.

1The exact component
The high-priority label, track side, zone, and component ID establish which brake actuator needs review.
2What has changed
A plain-language summary explains that the brake is responding more slowly. The latest reading of 420 ms sits beside the normal 280–340 ms range, with the 80 ms difference called out directly.
3Urgency and the maintenance window
The estimated downtime risk appears alongside tonight's 23:00–05:00 window, bringing condition and timing into the same view.
4The trend behind the alert
The six-hour chart shows how response time has changed. A shaded normal range makes the departure visible, and the sensor readings are available for closer review.
5A choice of next steps
Create a work request with the evidence attached, or escalate for immediate engineering review.

From evidence to action

Carry the context into the work request

The engineer can move from reviewing the alert to proposing work without rebuilding the component's story in a separate message.

1Component details are already there
The actuator name, ride location, and component ID carry over from the alert, keeping the request tied to the asset under review.
2The evidence stays attached
Current readings, the normal range, and the estimated risk travel with the request. The engineer can expand the evidence summary to check the readings before submitting.
3The engineer defines the work
An editable description and notes provide a starting point for the inspection or maintenance needed. The engineer can adjust them to reflect their assessment.

Connected parts coordination

Request the part without starting over

Instead of opening a separate email to the parts team, the engineer continues from the submitted work request with the component context already in place.

1The work request stays linked
The confirmation and request ID show that the work request is saved. The engineer can request a part now or skip this step without losing it.
2The suggested part is visible
A photo, part name, and catalog reference identify the suggested replacement alongside the affected component.
3Delivery needs are captured
Quantity, needed-by time, and delivery location give the parts team the details needed to coordinate the request around the proposed overnight maintenance window.
4Availability stays explicit
A pending status shows that compatibility, stock, and delivery timing still need confirmation from the parts team.

Pilot and learnings

Designing around the engineer’s next decision.

The original project progressed to pilot with Disney. The on-site research established the design direction: engineers needed to check component health while moving through the ride environment, understand where an issue was developing, and have enough evidence to determine the appropriate response.

The experience presented here develops that direction into a connected maintenance workflow. Component details and health evidence carry into work and parts requests, keeping the issue, proposed timing, and pending confirmations together as the response moves between teams.

What I learned

My main lesson was that better visibility only matters when it supports a decision. An alert needs to sit beside the component’s location, current readings, and trend so the engineer can assess the issue and choose a response. The interface should make that assessment easier while leaving urgency and repair decisions with the engineer.

I also learned to treat the handoff as part of the experience. The evidence that explains an alert should travel with the work request, and a parts request should stay connected to the same component. Clear status labels help teams distinguish what has been requested, what still needs confirmation, and what is ready to move forward.
