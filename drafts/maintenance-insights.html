Hitachi · Disney ride maintenance

From reactive alerts to planned maintenance.

I led the UX design for an experience intended to help engineers identify emerging ride issues and plan work before they disrupted park operations.

The problem

Engineers needed a chance to act before the ride stopped.

During our Disney visit, the engineer we spoke with described a reactive workflow. The system sent email notifications when something went wrong. Engineers needed to check their inbox, then investigate the affected ride area to identify the problem.

Downtime interrupted operations, contributed to longer queues, and frustrated guests. The opportunity was to support earlier investigation and, where engineering judgment allowed, plan maintenance overnight rather than respond during park hours.

The observed workflow began with a breakdown notification. The proposed experience gives engineers earlier context for a maintenance decision.

Problem anatomy

The email started an investigation, not a maintenance plan.

Limited sensor coverage meant a notification identified an affected ride area without identifying the exact faulty component. Email delivery also depended on network connectivity.

  1. Check email. Discover a notification after an issue occurs.
  2. Investigate the area. Find the specific component behind the broad location.
  3. Determine the response. Establish the work needed through investigation.
  4. Email the parts team. Request replacement parts separately.

Reconstructed from the research account, not an original email artifact or a description of Disney's operations today. A dashboard alone would not solve network reliability.

Field research

Design around the engineer's next decision.

I led research and persona interviews, including an on-site visit to Disney where we spoke with an engineer about daily routines and the information they monitored.

The research shaped two connected questions: what needs attention, and what needs to happen next.

Design challenge

Make the next step clear. Keep the engineer in control.

More sensors could provide more data, but not an automatic answer. The experience needed to connect the affected component, evidence of change, and a practical next step.

A prediction must not look like a guaranteed failure time. Proposed overnight work is not an approved schedule, and requesting a part does not confirm availability. The design needed to make those boundaries clear while helping engineers coordinate the response.

Design decisions

Four choices shaped the experience.

01

Put the component in context

A ride map and prioritized alerts connect the problem to its location and component ID.

02

Lead with the change

Show the latest reading against its normal range, then explain the trend and estimated downtime risk.

03

Keep timing subject to review

Place the overnight window alongside an escalation path. Engineers retain responsibility for urgency and operating decisions.

04

Keep work and parts connected

Link the component, work, and suggested part while separating engineering review from parts confirmation.

These choices describe the refreshed prototype, which revisits the original pilot experience and extends the parts-coordination flow.

Behind the design

The details that support an engineer's next decision

At-a-glance context

Put what needs attention beside the next maintenance window

I grouped monitoring coverage, issues needing review, vehicle activity, and the next overnight window into one summary strip. Engineers can orient themselves before looking into a specific component.

Neutral counts provide context, red emphasizes issues needing attention, and blue distinguishes the planning window. Labels and the priority breakdown make those differences clear without relying on color alone.

Location and priority

Connect the alert to its location

The original email identified a broad ride area. In the proposed experience, the prioritized alert list connects each issue to a named component, track side, and zone. The high-priority brake actuator appears first, giving the engineer a clear starting point for review.

Matching component markers connect the list to the ride schematic. I placed the callouts outside the track, with lines pointing to each component, so labels do not obscure the layout. Priority labels and icons reinforce the color cues.

Understanding the issue

Give the engineer evidence for the next decision

The drawer organizes the evidence around the engineer's assessment, from identifying the component to choosing a response.

The exact component
The high-priority label, track side, zone, and component ID establish which brake actuator needs review.
What has changed
A plain-language summary explains that the brake is responding more slowly. The latest reading of 420 ms sits beside the normal 280–340 ms range, with the 80 ms difference called out directly.
Urgency and the maintenance window
The estimated downtime risk appears alongside tonight's 23:00–05:00 window. This gives the engineer planning context, not permission to wait: inspection and engineering judgment still determine the response.
The trend behind the alert
The six-hour chart shows how response time has changed. A shaded normal range makes the departure visible, and the sensor readings are available for closer review.
A choice of next steps
Create a work request with the evidence attached, or escalate for immediate engineering review.

From evidence to action

Carry the context into the work request

The engineer can move from reviewing the alert to proposing work without rebuilding the component's story in a separate message.

Component details are already there
The actuator name, ride location, and component ID carry over from the alert, keeping the request tied to the asset under review.
The evidence stays attached
Current readings, the normal range, and the estimated risk travel with the request. The engineer can expand the evidence summary to check the readings before submitting.
The engineer defines the work
An editable description and notes provide a starting point for the inspection or maintenance needed. The engineer can adjust them to reflect their assessment.
Timing is proposed, not promised
The overnight window is a proposal for engineering review, not a confirmed repair schedule.

Connected parts coordination

Request the part without starting over

Instead of opening a separate email to the parts team, the engineer continues from the submitted work request with the component context already in place.

The work request stays linked
The confirmation and request ID show that the work request is saved. The engineer can request a part now or skip this step without losing it.
The suggested part is visible
A photo, part name, and catalog reference identify the suggested replacement alongside the affected component. The suggestion still needs compatibility confirmation from the parts team.
Delivery needs are captured
Quantity, needed-by time, and delivery location give the parts team the details needed to coordinate the request around the proposed overnight maintenance window.
Availability stays explicit
The pending message separates requesting a part from securing it. Submission does not reserve stock or confirm the repair schedule; the parts team still verifies compatibility, availability, and delivery timing.

Pilot and learnings

Earlier insight needs a clear path to action.

This work was developed with Disney for a ride-maintenance pilot. As lead UX designer, I led the on-site research, engineer interviews, workflows, and overall experience, partnering with a junior designer on production assets. The project progressed to pilot.

The research helped me see that earlier detection alone would not solve the problem. Engineers still needed to locate the affected component, understand what had changed, and coordinate the work and replacement parts. The design needed to support that entire response, not just deliver another alert.

That insight shaped the experience: connect component health to a clear next step, carry the evidence into work requests, and support planning for the overnight maintenance window. The goal was to help engineers address issues before they interrupted park hours, while keeping decisions about urgency and repairs in their hands.

The project reached pilot; measured operational results were not available for this case study. Demonstrations use illustrative data.
